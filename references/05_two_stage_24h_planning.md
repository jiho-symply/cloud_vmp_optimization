# Two-Stage Stochastic Planning and a 24-Hour Horizon

## Claim

A two-stage stochastic temporal placement model with a 24-hour, time-indexed horizon is a defensible abstraction for studying cloud and data-center operations when the research question is about day-ahead consolidation, energy-aware server activation, SLA risk, and recourse after uncertain demand is realized. The defensible claim is conditional: cloud operations are not inherently 24-hour problems, and production schedulers do not necessarily commit to a single day-ahead plan. A 24-hour horizon is appropriate when it represents a daily planning cycle, a day-scale workload or energy pattern, a batch/reservation service window, or a product-specific transient-VM lifetime rule.

The model should therefore be presented as a tractable research abstraction, not as a universal cloud-control architecture. Its strength is that it exposes the tradeoff between planned capacity and scenario-dependent recourse in a compact extensive-form MILP. Its limitation is that it compresses continuous observation, online arrivals, and repeated reoptimization into a small number of decision stages.

## How the Current Model Matches

The current model follows a standard two-stage stochastic-programming separation. The first stage commits to a baseline time-indexed plan: server activation, on-demand VM placement, spot or preemptible VM baseline placement, and managed-batch reservation decisions. The second stage evaluates each demand scenario and applies recourse: on-demand migration or overload accounting, lower-priority suspension, and scenario-specific batch processing on reserved server-time capacity.

The 24-hour horizon is consistent with this structure if it is interpreted as one daily operating cycle with hourly or otherwise discretized periods. It gives the model enough temporal structure to represent within-day load variation, server fire-up or shutdown decisions, priority-aware recourse, batch reservation windows, and preemption/suspension consequences, while keeping the stochastic MILP small enough to solve as a research prototype.

This matches the literature at the level of abstraction, not in every operational detail. Stochastic bin-packing work supports separating first-stage capacity plans from scenario-dependent recourse. Cloud stochastic-consolidation and service-planning work supports finite-horizon, time-indexed resource decisions with uncertain workloads and daily patterns. Temporal bin-packing and VMP work supports assigning jobs or VMs with time windows to server capacity over a planning horizon. Online VM-allocation and model-predictive-control work show important alternatives: many production systems make placement decisions continuously, with predictions and repeated replanning rather than a single two-stage day-ahead commitment.

## Modeling Distinctions

### Two-stage stochastic planning

In a two-stage model, first-stage decisions are made before the scenario is known, and second-stage recourse is made after uncertainty is revealed. For the current formulation, this means that server activation, baseline placement, and reservation decisions are nonanticipative across all scenarios, while migration, suspension, overload accounting, and batch execution can vary by scenario.

This is the right abstraction when the paper wants to study the value of planned capacity under uncertainty: how many servers should be activated, how much batch capacity should be reserved, and how much operational recourse is needed when realized usage differs from the forecast. Crainic et al. provide the clean bin-packing analogue: capacity units are selected first, and scenario-specific extra capacity and packing decisions occur later. Shen and Wang provide the cloud analogue: server consolidation and switching decisions are optimized under uncertain workloads, with scenario-based MIPs, chance constraints, and a benchmark two-stage stochastic program with recourse scheduling.

The limitation is that two-stage planning treats information arrival coarsely. If the scheduler observes demand every few minutes and can revise placements throughout the day, then a pure two-stage model is a simplification unless it is embedded in a rolling-horizon implementation.

### Multi-stage stochastic planning

In a multi-stage stochastic model, decisions are made at several decision epochs as uncertainty is gradually revealed. Nonanticipativity is imposed by information history: scenarios that share the same past must share the same decisions up to that point, but may diverge later. Bulbul et al. show this structure for cloud resource provisioning with reservations, on-demand procurement, and chance constraints over a tactical horizon.

This is more realistic when capacity commitments, procurement, or placement decisions can be adjusted repeatedly during the day or over multiple days. The cost is scenario-tree growth. A multi-stage formulation may represent operational learning better than the current two-stage model, but it can be much harder to solve at VM-placement scale.

### Online allocation

Online models place VMs as requests arrive, often without knowing future requests exactly. Their performance is usually evaluated through competitive analysis, simulation, or production metrics rather than an extensive-form scenario objective. Cohen et al. model cloud overcommitment as online bin packing with chance constraints. Buchbinder et al. explicitly model VM allocation as dynamic bin packing and study the value of lifetime and aggregate-load predictions. Barbalho et al. describe a production Azure allocator that uses ML lifetime predictions in the critical path for dynamic VM allocation.

Online models are the strongest counterpoint to a 24-hour two-stage formulation. They are more natural for immediate VM admission and millisecond-scale allocation systems. However, they do not replace day-ahead or horizon-based models when the research question is capacity reservation, energy-aware activation, or batch-service planning over a known service window.

### Rolling horizon and model predictive control

Rolling-horizon or model-predictive-control (MPC) models repeatedly solve a finite-horizon optimization problem, implement only the first decision or first few decisions, then update the forecast and solve again. Gaggero and Caviglione apply MPC to VM placement with energy, quality, and security objectives, which is direct evidence that VM placement can be studied as a repeated finite-horizon control problem.

This approach is often more operationally realistic than solving one 24-hour extensive form once. It handles boundary effects, forecast updates, and continuous operation better. It can also use a 24-hour look-ahead horizon without claiming that the system is committed for 24 hours. If the current model is used in a rolling-horizon wrapper, the 24-hour horizon becomes easier to defend: it is a look-ahead window, not a one-shot universal commitment.

## Paper-Specific Synthesis

The strongest direct support for a two-stage stochastic planning structure comes from Crainic et al. and Shen and Wang. Crainic et al. are not cloud-specific, but their first-stage capacity and second-stage recourse split is exactly the type of operations-research abstraction used by the current model. Shen and Wang make the same idea cloud-specific by optimizing server consolidation, energy, service quality, and recourse scheduling under uncertain workloads. Together, they justify the current model's planned server-time capacity plus scenario recourse structure.

The strongest direct support for temporal placement constraints comes from de Cauwer et al., Dell'Amico et al., and Aydin et al. These papers support the idea that a workload or VM consumes capacity only over its active interval, and that minimizing active bins/servers and activation events is a legitimate temporal packing objective. They do not, by themselves, justify stochastic demand scenarios. That stochastic extension must be supported by Crainic et al., Zhang et al., Cohen et al., and Shen and Wang.

The 24-hour horizon has narrower support. Shen and Wang discuss daily workload patterns, Youssef and Krishnamurthy explicitly use a 24-hour planning horizon with 30-minute allocation intervals, and SciSpot gives a product-specific case where a 24-hour transient-VM constraint was central. These papers justify 24 hours as a plausible daily or product-semantic horizon. They do not justify saying that cloud VMP is naturally 24-hour in general.

The best counterexamples are Bulbul et al., Gaggero and Caviglione, Buchbinder et al., and Barbalho et al. Bulbul et al. show that cloud resource planning can require multi-stage stochastic adaptation. Gaggero and Caviglione show that VM placement can be modeled as rolling-horizon/MPC control. Buchbinder et al. and Barbalho et al. show that online dynamic bin packing with predictions is a credible production-style VM allocation model. These alternatives do not invalidate the current model, but they require the paper to state its scope carefully.

## Different Modeling Choices in the Literature

- Two-stage stochastic bin packing and capacity planning: Crainic et al. use a first-stage capacity plan and second-stage recourse under demand scenarios. This is close to the current model's stochastic-programming logic, but it is not cloud-specific.
- Stochastic cloud consolidation over a finite horizon: Shen and Wang model uncertain workloads, expected energy, server switching, chance constraints, and two-stage recourse scheduling. This is close in spirit to energy-aware stochastic placement, but it abstracts away explicit VM time windows.
- Deterministic temporal bin packing: de Cauwer et al. and Dell'Amico et al. treat item activity windows as known and enforce capacity at each instant. This is appropriate for reservation-style or scheduled jobs, but it does not capture demand uncertainty unless combined with stochastic scenarios.
- Cloud VMP as temporal bin packing with energy/fire-up objectives: Aydin et al. make the cloud connection explicit and add objectives related to active servers and fire-ups. This supports the current model's energy-aware temporal placement objective but leaves stochastic recourse as an extension.
- Chance-constrained stochastic packing: Zhang et al. and Cohen et al. support modeling item/resource usage uncertainty through chance constraints. Zhang et al. provide a two-stage stochastic bin-packing formulation; Cohen et al. provide a cloud overcommitment model with online arrivals.
- Multi-stage stochastic provisioning: Bulbul et al. show that cloud resource planning may need several decision stages when information and procurement decisions evolve over time. This is a credible alternative when the system can reoptimize frequently or when contracts extend beyond a day.
- Rolling-horizon/MPC placement: Gaggero and Caviglione show that VM placement can be optimized repeatedly over a prediction horizon. This is the most natural way to make a 24-hour look-ahead horizon operational without claiming a one-shot daily commitment.
- Online dynamic VM allocation: Buchbinder et al. and Barbalho et al. model VM allocation as dynamic bin packing with lifetime/load predictions. These works are closer to production admission and placement loops than a day-ahead stochastic MILP.
- Explicit 24-hour service-planning examples: Youssef and Krishnamurthy use a 24-hour planning horizon with 30-minute resource-allocation intervals for enterprise application clouds. SciSpot uses 24-hour preemptible-VM semantics as part of the system model. These are examples, not universal rules.

## Caveats

The 24-hour horizon should be presented as a modeling choice. It is strongest when the trace, forecast, batch-service contract, operational review process, electricity/SLO accounting cycle, or VM product semantics have a daily structure. It is weaker for workloads with weekly seasonality, long-running services, multi-day batch jobs, sub-hour autoscaling dynamics, or spot products without a 24-hour expiration.

A fixed daily horizon introduces boundary effects. VMs or batch jobs active across midnight need carry-over state, terminal penalties, or a rolling-horizon implementation. Otherwise the model can make end-of-horizon decisions that would be unattractive in continuous operations.

Hourly discretization is also a simplification. It is useful for tractability, but it can miss short spikes, short preemption notices, fast autoscaling events, and migration transients. Finer intervals increase model size; coarser intervals can understate SLA risk.

The current model's two-stage structure is appropriate for studying planned capacity plus scenario recourse, but it is not the only defensible stochastic abstraction. Multi-stage, robust, online, or model-predictive formulations may be better when new observations arrive continuously and decisions can be revised throughout the day.

Finally, the 24-hour argument does not remove the other modeling caveats in the current prototype. A scalar CPU capacity abstraction, simplified migration/suspension recourse, and stylized priority classes are useful for isolating stochastic temporal placement tradeoffs, but they are not a complete production scheduler.

## Recommended Wording for the Paper

Use a conditional statement such as:

> We model provider-side VM placement as a two-stage stochastic temporal bin-packing problem over a 24-hour planning horizon. The first stage represents day-ahead server activation, baseline placement, and managed-batch reservation decisions; the second stage represents scenario-dependent recourse after uncertain VM usage and workload demand are realized. The 24-hour horizon is used as a daily planning/look-ahead window, consistent with prior cloud service-planning studies and with some transient-VM semantics, but it is not intended to describe all cloud scheduling systems. Online, multi-stage, and rolling-horizon formulations are natural alternatives when placement decisions are revised continuously.

Avoid wording such as:

> Cloud VM placement is naturally a 24-hour two-stage stochastic program.

That stronger statement is not supported by the literature. The supported statement is that a 24-hour two-stage formulation is a reasonable prototype abstraction for day-ahead or look-ahead risk-aware placement, especially if the paper clearly labels online and rolling-horizon operation as alternatives.

## Verdict

The current model can be defensibly described as a two-stage stochastic temporal VMP/TBPP prototype over a 24-hour planning horizon. The strongest formulation is: a daily horizon is a tractable and literature-consistent abstraction for studying risk-aware consolidation, priority-aware recourse, managed-batch reservation, and energy-aware activation under within-day uncertainty. The model should not claim that 24 hours is universal. It should state that 24 hours is chosen because it is operationally meaningful for daily planning, batch service windows, or some preemptible-VM semantics, and because it keeps the stochastic temporal MILP interpretable and solvable.
